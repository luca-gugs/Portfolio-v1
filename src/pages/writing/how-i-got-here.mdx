I studied philosophy at NYU. I loved the coursework and burned out on it anyway. The philosophy majors I knew were headed for teaching or law, and I couldn't picture myself doing either.

So I tried commercial real estate. I was terrible at it. I was very shy back then (less so now), and it would take me hours to work up the courage for a single cold call. Hours. Then I'd dial, get rejected immediately, and go cry in the bathroom.

Then my friends and I decided to build our own version of CoStar. You may notice a problem with this plan, which is that none of us could code. We noticed it too. Our solution was to pay a “highly touted” overseas dev team to build it for us. They stole our money.

After that, with our pride and our wallets both lighter, we set about teaching ourselves. PHP first (mistake), then JavaScript, which had us from the first time we got the command line to tell us the weather. We'd cook up startup ideas in our living room in the East Village and I'd build a dumb website for each one. The funniest, looking back, was a LinkedIn for politicians, where voters could read a local candidate's platform and leave feedback on it. This was 2016, peak social media meets peak politics. (The irony is that I'm about to have a daughter, and I've never cared more about my town's politics.)

In 2019 an engineer at EasyKnock, a proptech startup in NYC, got me in the door. Nobody was going to hand the new hire anything important, and nobody did. I got what I'd call “React for babies”: styled-components (remember those?) and basic forms. So I begged. For the pricing engine, for the internal tools our sales, underwriting, and portfolio teams lived in, for any ticket harder than the one I had. Somewhere in all that begging I learned to review code and be a good teammate. I loved listening to the senior developers argue over how to build something within the constraints we had. I loved watching something I shipped make someone's workday a little easier. It's cliche, sure, but I was hooked.

At some point I had an idea for a portal that would walk customers through trading their mortgage for a lease and cash. I built it on nights and weekends and pitched it to the CEO and CPO, and they bought in. We pulled in the sharpest PM I've ever met and a great designer, and ran it like a little startup inside the company. The Salesforce admins fought it. Their position was that all of this should keep living in a CRM. They were comically wrong. I should have fought them harder. I was young and scared for my job, and I didn't. Thankfully a few people stuck up for us.

After five years I wanted to be on a small, fast team where I could have a larger impact and a greater voice. In 2024 I joined RetailReady as the first hire. It has been two years. In that time I've helped build a supply chain company that has shipped over 100k orders and nearly 4 million cartons. That is a shitload of cardboard.
